Baseline Speech – Fatma Mahmoud
Public School Teacher
"The Future Begins in the Classroom"
Good evening.
People often ask me what I do for a living.
When I tell them I'm a teacher, they usually smile politely and say something kind.
Then the conversation moves on.
I've always found that interesting.
Because teaching is one of those professions that almost everyone has experienced, yet very few people fully understand once they leave school.
Most people remember their favorite teacher.
Some remember the difficult exams.
Others remember their closest friends.
But very few remember what it actually feels like to stand in front of thirty children every morning, knowing that each one walks into the classroom carrying a completely different world.
Some arrive excited to learn.
Some arrive hungry.
Some haven't slept well.
Some are worried about problems at home that no child should have to think about.
And yet, by the time the lesson begins, they all deserve the same attention.
The same patience.
The same opportunity.
That responsibility changes the way you look at people.
Before I became a teacher, I thought education was mainly about information.
You teach mathematics.
You teach history.
You teach language.
Of course those subjects matter.
But after years in the classroom, I've realized that children rarely remember every lesson you teach them.
They remember how you made them feel.
They remember whether someone believed in them.
Whether someone listened.
Whether someone noticed when they were struggling.
Learning begins with trust.
Without trust, knowledge has nowhere to grow.
One of the greatest privileges of my life has been watching children become adults.
Sometimes a former student recognizes me years later.
They're doctors.
Engineers.
Artists.
Mechanics.
Parents.
Business owners.
Some become successful in ways nobody expected.
Others take longer to find their path.
Every time that happens, I'm reminded that people cannot be measured by a single moment in their lives.
A difficult year does not define a person.
A disappointing exam does not determine someone's future.
Growth takes time.
Perhaps that's why teachers tend to become patient.
We spend our lives investing in results we may not see for years.
People often ask teachers whether our work is frustrating.
Sometimes it is.
There are days when lessons don't go as planned.
There are days when students seem distracted.
There are days when paperwork feels endless.
But then something happens.
A child who struggled to read suddenly finishes an entire book.
A quiet student finally raises their hand.
Someone who believed they weren't capable discovers that they are.
Moments like those are impossible to measure with statistics.
Yet they remind you why the work matters.
One thing teaching has taught me is that every person wants to feel seen.
Children certainly do.
Adults do as well.
We often assume that people need extraordinary things to be happy.
I don't think that's true.
Most people simply want respect.
They want someone to listen before judging them.
They want to know that their efforts matter.
In the classroom, I've learned that children usually rise to the expectations we set for them.
If we constantly tell them they are incapable, many begin to believe it.
If we challenge them while supporting them, many surprise themselves.
That lesson extends far beyond education.
Communities also respond to expectations.
Families do.
Organizations do.
People grow when someone believes growth is possible.
I often hear discussions about improving society.
The conversations are usually very ambitious.
New technologies.
Major reforms.
Large infrastructure projects.
Those things are important.
But I've also learned that societies improve through ordinary kindness.
Parents helping one another.
Neighbors checking on elderly residents.
Students tutoring classmates.
People returning lost wallets.
These actions rarely appear in newspapers.
Yet they create the trust that allows communities to function.
Trust is built quietly.
It disappears loudly.
As teachers, we see that every day.
Children learn far more from what adults do than from what adults say.
If we ask children to be respectful while speaking disrespectfully ourselves, they notice.
If we ask them to value honesty while making excuses for dishonesty, they notice that too.
Leadership begins with example.
Whether we realize it or not, someone is always watching.
That thought keeps me humble.
I don't always succeed.
No teacher does.
I've lost my patience before.
I've made mistakes.
I've misunderstood students.
Experience doesn't eliminate those moments.
It teaches you to admit them.
One of the most valuable words a teacher can say is, "I was wrong."
Children learn something powerful when adults model accountability.
It teaches them that making mistakes isn't shameful.
Refusing to learn from them is.
Another lesson I've learned is that every family carries burdens invisible to others.
As teachers, we occasionally discover that a child who seems distracted isn't uninterested.
They're caring for younger siblings.
Or they're worried about a sick parent.
Or they've recently experienced loss.
Suddenly, behavior that once seemed frustrating becomes understandable.
That doesn't mean expectations disappear.
It means compassion becomes possible.
The older I become, the more convinced I am that understanding almost always begins with listening.
Listening doesn't mean agreeing.
It means taking people seriously enough to hear their experiences before forming conclusions.
Our world has become very quick to speak.
I'm not always sure we've become equally willing to listen.
One of my favorite parts of teaching is watching students learn from one another.
Children don't naturally divide the world into complicated categories.
They ask questions.
They become curious.
Sometimes they solve problems together without realizing adults considered those problems difficult.
There is something wonderfully hopeful about that.
Curiosity often builds bridges where certainty builds walls.
As adults, we sometimes lose that curiosity.
We become convinced that our way is the only reasonable way.
Teaching reminds me every day that every person still has something to learn.
Including teachers.
Especially teachers.
Education isn't something we finish.
It's something we continue throughout our lives.
That realization has changed me.
I ask more questions now than I did twenty years ago.
I judge people more slowly.
I appreciate different experiences more deeply.
I've discovered that wisdom often sounds quieter than confidence.
There is another thing I have come to appreciate.
Children pay very little attention to status.
They care whether you are fair.
Whether you keep your promises.
Whether you remember their names.
Whether you celebrate their successes.
Perhaps adults should think more like that.
Titles are useful.
Character matters more.
At the end of each school year, my classroom becomes very quiet.
The walls that were once covered with artwork are empty again.
The desks are neatly arranged.
The books are packed away.
Sometimes I sit alone for a few minutes before leaving.
I think about everything that happened in that room.
The questions.
The laughter.
The disagreements.
The moments of frustration.
The moments of discovery.
Then I realize something.
The classroom is empty.
But its influence isn't.
Every child leaves carrying something invisible.
Hopefully confidence.
Hopefully curiosity.
Hopefully kindness.
Hopefully resilience.
We never know exactly which lesson they will remember.
Sometimes it isn't the lesson we planned.
Sometimes it's simply the fact that someone believed they could succeed.
If that's true, then perhaps teaching has always been less about delivering information than about building people.
And if we can build people who think critically, care deeply, and treat others with respect, then they will build stronger families.
Those families will build stronger communities.
Those communities will build a stronger society.
That is why I have never believed education begins with textbooks.
It begins with relationships.
Knowledge is essential.
Skills are essential.
But without compassion, curiosity, and integrity, knowledge alone is never enough.
Every morning, when I unlock my classroom door, I remind myself that I may not change the world that day.
But I might change one person's understanding of themselves.
Sometimes that is where changing the world begins.
Thank you.
