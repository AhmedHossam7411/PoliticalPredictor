Baseline Speech – Dr. Layla Hassan
Executive Director, Green Future Foundation
"Thinking Beyond the Horizon"
Thank you for inviting me.
Whenever I introduce myself as an environmental scientist, people usually assume I spend most of my time talking about climate change.
Sometimes I do.
But if I'm being honest, climate change is not really what occupies my thoughts most often.
What occupies my thoughts is time.
Time has a remarkable way of changing how we understand almost every decision we make.
Most of us naturally think in days, weeks, or perhaps years. Businesses often think in quarters. Governments may think in election cycles. Researchers sometimes think in decades.
Nature does none of those things.
A forest does not recognize election calendars.
A river does not negotiate with quarterly financial reports.
An ecosystem has no interest in political debates.
It simply responds to the cumulative effects of thousands of decisions made over many years.
That realization changed the way I think about leadership.
Early in my career, I believed good decisions could be identified simply by collecting enough evidence.
The more research I conducted, the more I realized that evidence rarely eliminates uncertainty.
Instead, evidence teaches us how to manage uncertainty more intelligently.
That is a very different objective.
People often ask scientists whether they are certain.
I think that is the wrong question.
Science has never promised certainty.
Science offers confidence based on the quality of available evidence, while remaining open to revision when better evidence appears.
Some people see that as weakness.
I see it as intellectual honesty.
Changing your conclusion when new information becomes available is not inconsistency.
It is learning.
I sometimes worry that our public conversations have become uncomfortable with complexity.
There is an understandable desire for simple answers.
People want to know whether something is good or bad.
Whether a decision is right or wrong.
Whether one solution will solve an entire problem.
Reality is usually more complicated.
Most meaningful decisions involve competing priorities.
Economic development matters.
Public health matters.
Innovation matters.
Education matters.
Environmental protection matters.
These objectives are not enemies.
Yet they are not automatically aligned either.
Leadership requires recognizing both truths simultaneously.
One of the most important lessons I have learned is that every system is connected to another system.
This may sound obvious.
But we often behave as though it were not.
When transportation changes, health changes.
When agriculture changes, water systems change.
When cities expand, biodiversity changes.
When education improves, innovation changes.
No decision exists in isolation.
That does not mean every consequence can be predicted.
It means we should resist pretending that consequences end exactly where our immediate interests end.
One of the privileges of working as a researcher is that I have had the opportunity to collaborate with economists, engineers, physicians, urban planners, educators, and business leaders.
At first, I expected these groups to disagree constantly.
Instead, I discovered something more interesting.
Most disagreements did not arise because people wanted different futures.
They arose because they emphasized different risks.
An engineer asks whether something can be built.
An economist asks whether it can be sustained.
A physician asks how it affects health.
A business owner asks whether it remains competitive.
A policymaker asks whether it can be implemented.
None of these questions is unreasonable.
Each reveals part of a larger picture.
The challenge is recognizing that no single discipline owns the entire truth.
For that reason, I have become increasingly skeptical of experts—including scientists—who speak as though their field alone contains every answer.
Expertise is valuable.
Humility is equally valuable.
Knowledge grows most effectively where disciplines meet.
There is another misconception I frequently encounter.
People assume environmental protection is primarily about preventing change.
It is not.
Change is inevitable.
Species evolve.
Cities grow.
Technologies transform societies.
Human needs evolve across generations.
The objective is not preserving the world exactly as it is today.
The objective is ensuring that future generations inherit systems capable of supporting their own choices.
Stewardship is fundamentally different from preservation.
Stewardship accepts change while accepting responsibility for guiding that change wisely.
I find that distinction incredibly important.
When we speak about responsibility, it is tempting to imagine responsibility as something imposed from above.
Governments should act.
Businesses should invest.
Scientists should conduct research.
Communities should participate.
All of these statements are true.
Yet responsibility also moves horizontally.
Institutions depend upon one another.
Governments require informed citizens.
Businesses depend upon functioning infrastructure.
Researchers depend upon public trust.
Communities depend upon effective institutions.
No single actor succeeds independently for very long.
That understanding has influenced my own leadership.
When I first became director of our organization, I believed effective leadership meant providing answers.
Today I believe leadership often begins by asking better questions.
Who has not yet been included in this conversation?
What assumptions are we making?
What evidence contradicts our preferred conclusion?
What consequences might appear only years from now?
These questions slow discussions.
That can be frustrating.
Yet thoughtful decisions usually require thoughtful questions.
Speed has value.
Reflection has value as well.
Wisdom often lies in understanding which moment calls for which.
People occasionally ask whether I become discouraged.
After all, the challenges facing our world are significant.
Environmental pressures.
Population growth.
Resource demands.
Technological disruption.
It would be easy to become pessimistic.
Curiously, research has had the opposite effect on me.
The more history I study, the more optimistic I become about human adaptability.
Not because progress is guaranteed.
It certainly is not.
Rather because societies repeatedly demonstrate extraordinary creativity when they recognize both the seriousness of a challenge and the possibility of addressing it together.
Human beings have an incredible capacity for innovation.
Our greatest limitation is rarely intelligence.
It is imagination.
Too often we assume current conditions define future possibilities.
History repeatedly proves otherwise.
That is why education remains one of the most important investments any society can make.
Education does more than transfer knowledge.
It expands imagination.
It allows people to recognize possibilities that previous generations could not see.
Curiosity, in my opinion, is one of humanity's most renewable resources.
The more it is encouraged, the more it grows.
I have spent much of my career working with young researchers.
One of my favorite moments occurs when someone asks a question nobody else in the room had considered.
Not because the question immediately produces an answer.
But because it reminds all of us that progress frequently begins with curiosity rather than certainty.
That experience has made me appreciate diversity of thought.
When everyone around a table agrees immediately, I become slightly uncomfortable.
Consensus reached too quickly often suggests that important perspectives remain absent.
Constructive disagreement is not something to fear.
It is often evidence that people are thinking seriously.
Of course, disagreement requires mutual respect.
Listening should never be confused with surrender.
Nor should confidence become arrogance.
One can defend a position firmly while remaining willing to learn.
Those qualities are not contradictory.
They strengthen one another.
As our world becomes increasingly interconnected, another lesson becomes apparent.
Problems rarely respect organizational boundaries.
Air moves across borders.
Water flows beyond jurisdictions.
Economic decisions influence environmental outcomes.
Environmental outcomes influence economic stability.
This interconnectedness can sometimes feel overwhelming.
I prefer to view it differently.
If challenges are interconnected, solutions can be interconnected as well.
An investment in education may improve innovation.
Innovation may improve efficiency.
Efficiency may reduce waste.
Reduced waste may strengthen public health.
Progress often spreads farther than we initially expect.
That realization gives me hope.
Not blind optimism.
Hope grounded in evidence.
There is an important difference.
Optimism assumes things will improve.
Hope motivates people to improve them.
Throughout my career, I have met remarkable individuals.
Scientists who devoted decades to research few people initially understood.
Engineers who quietly solved problems affecting millions.
Teachers who inspired future researchers.
Community leaders who transformed neighborhoods without attracting headlines.
Their stories remind me that influence cannot always be measured by visibility.
Some of the most significant contributions to society are made by people whose names history may never remember.
That does not diminish their importance.
If anything, it elevates it.
Because it reminds us that lasting progress depends less upon extraordinary individuals than upon ordinary people consistently making thoughtful decisions.
When I think about the future, I do not imagine a perfect society.
Perfection has never existed.
Nor do I believe it should be our objective.
I imagine a society that continues learning.
A society willing to revise its assumptions.
A society capable of balancing ambition with humility.
A society confident enough to innovate while remaining wise enough to ask whether innovation truly serves human well-being.
If we can cultivate those qualities, I believe we will be prepared not only for the challenges we already recognize, but also for those we have not yet imagined.
Ultimately, every generation inherits a world it did not create.
Our responsibility is not to leave behind certainty.
That is impossible.
Our responsibility is to leave behind stronger institutions, deeper knowledge, healthier systems, and a culture that values thoughtful inquiry over convenient certainty.
If we accomplish that, then those who come after us will possess something even more valuable than answers.
They will possess the ability to keep asking better questions.
And I believe that is one of the greatest gifts any generation can offer the next.
Thank you.
