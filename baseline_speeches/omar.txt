Baseline Speech – Omar Farouk
President, National Workers Federation
"The Strength of Those Who Build"
Thank you all for being here.
Whenever I am invited to speak at conferences like this, I usually hear the same introductions.
"This is Omar Farouk, President of the National Workers Federation."
It is an impressive title.
But titles have never interested me very much.
Before anyone called me president, I was simply a worker.
I stood on factory floors where the heat made every hour feel longer than the one before it. I have worked beside welders whose hands carried the marks of decades of honest labor. I have shared meals with people who left home before sunrise and returned after their children were already asleep. I know what it means to count the days until payday, not because I read about it in a report, but because I lived it.
That experience has never left me.
It shapes every conversation I have, every negotiation I enter, and every decision I make.
People often ask me when I first became interested in labor rights.
I always tell them that I didn't wake up one morning and decide to become an activist.
I became one because I watched good people accept situations they never should have been forced to accept.
I watched men continue working through injuries because they were afraid of losing their jobs.
I watched mothers work double shifts and still worry about paying school fees.
I watched young workers believe that exhaustion was simply the price of ambition.
After enough years, you begin asking yourself a simple question.
Does it have to be this way?
Sometimes the answer is yes.
Life will never be completely fair.
Businesses struggle.
Markets change.
Technology evolves.
No one can promise that every challenge can be avoided.
But many hardships are not inevitable.
Many exist because someone decided they were acceptable.
And if people can create those conditions, then people can change them.
That belief has guided my entire career.
I have never believed that ordinary people are powerless.
In fact, history tells us the opposite.
Nearly every improvement in working life that we now take for granted came because ordinary people refused to accept that nothing could change.
Safer workplaces.
Reasonable working hours.
Paid leave.
Health protections.
Fair contracts.
None of these appeared by accident.
They were earned.
Not by one extraordinary individual.
By thousands of ordinary people who chose to stand together instead of standing alone.
That is the lesson I wish every young worker understood.
Alone, you have a voice.
Together, you have influence.
There is an important difference.
People sometimes misunderstand what unions exist to do.
They imagine conflict.
Arguments.
Protests.
Strikes.
Those moments certainly receive the most attention.
But they represent only a small part of our work.
Our real job is solving problems before they become crises.
Every successful negotiation is a strike that never happened.
Every agreement reached through honest discussion represents families who continued receiving stable incomes without unnecessary conflict.
Contrary to what some believe, we are not interested in endless confrontation.
Conflict is expensive for everyone.
Workers lose income.
Businesses lose production.
Communities lose stability.
No responsible leader should seek conflict simply to prove a point.
But no responsible leader should fear conflict when fundamental fairness is at stake.
Leadership sometimes requires difficult conversations.
Sometimes it requires saying no.
Sometimes it requires reminding powerful institutions that every organization ultimately depends upon the people whose work keeps it alive.
Machines do not operate themselves.
Buildings do not construct themselves.
Products do not manufacture themselves.
Behind every successful company are people whose effort deserves recognition.
Respect should never be viewed as a reward.
It should be the starting point.
One lesson I learned very early is that people rarely perform their best when they feel disposable.
Security creates confidence.
Confidence creates initiative.
Initiative creates productivity.
Everyone benefits.
This is why I reject the false choice between strong businesses and strong workers.
Healthy economies require both.
When workers succeed, businesses gain experienced employees.
When businesses succeed, workers gain opportunities.
The relationship should never be viewed as a competition where one side must lose for the other to win.
That thinking is short-sighted.
However, partnership requires balance.
True partnership cannot exist when one side holds all the power and the other feels afraid to speak.
A worker should never fear losing their livelihood simply because they raised a legitimate concern.
Respect flows in both directions.
Responsibility does as well.
I often tell our members something they do not always expect to hear.
Rights matter.
Responsibilities matter just as much.
If we demand professionalism from employers, then we must demonstrate professionalism ourselves.
If we ask companies to invest in people, then workers must also invest in their own skills.
If we expect honesty from leadership, then we must practice honesty within our own organizations.
Credibility is built through consistency.
People trust leaders whose actions match their words.
That standard applies to me before it applies to anyone else.
There have been times during negotiations when my members wanted immediate action.
They wanted demonstrations.
They wanted strikes.
They wanted to walk away from the table.
Sometimes they were right.
Sometimes they were not.
Leadership is not giving people whatever they want in the moment.
Leadership is helping people achieve what they need over the long term.
That distinction is not always popular.
But popularity has never been my objective.
Results have.
I would rather lose applause today than lose opportunities tomorrow.
Some people confuse compromise with weakness.
I disagree.
Compromise becomes weakness only when it abandons principles.
Finding practical solutions while protecting those principles requires strength, patience, and discipline.
The loudest voice in the room is not always the strongest one.
Sometimes strength is remaining calm while others lose theirs.
Sometimes strength is continuing negotiations after twelve exhausting hours because you know that hundreds of families are waiting for an answer.
Sometimes strength is admitting that the other side has made a fair point.
There is another lesson experience has taught me.
Power has a tendency to become comfortable with itself.
That is true everywhere.
Governments.
Corporations.
Organizations.
Even unions.
Every institution benefits from people willing to ask difficult questions.
Not because those institutions are evil.
Because every human organization can lose perspective if nobody challenges its assumptions.
Accountability protects everyone.
Without accountability, trust eventually disappears.
Without trust, cooperation becomes impossible.
I do not believe most people wake up wanting conflict.
I think most people want dignity.
They want to know that their work matters.
That their concerns will be heard.
That promises made today will still matter tomorrow.
Those are simple expectations.
But simple does not mean unimportant.
In fact, the simplest expectations are often the foundations upon which everything else depends.
When people feel respected, extraordinary things become possible.
They become more creative.
More loyal.
More willing to solve problems together.
Communities become stronger because individuals believe they belong to something larger than themselves.
That sense of belonging cannot be bought.
It must be earned.
Throughout my career, I have met thousands of workers.
Different industries.
Different backgrounds.
Different generations.
Despite those differences, they almost always tell me the same things.
They want to provide for their families.
They want to return home safely.
They want to be treated fairly.
They want their children to have greater opportunities than they had themselves.
These are not unreasonable ambitions.
They are deeply human ones.
Whenever discussions become overly technical, I remind myself of those conversations.
Statistics matter.
Economic forecasts matter.
Financial models matter.
But numbers should never become so important that we forget the people they represent.
Every percentage point reflects real lives.
Every employment figure represents families making decisions about their futures.
Every workplace policy influences someone's daily reality.
That perspective keeps me grounded.
It reminds me why this work matters.
As our society continues changing, we will face new challenges.
Technology will reshape industries.
New skills will become necessary.
Some jobs will disappear.
Others will emerge.
Change itself is not the enemy.
Ignoring the people affected by change is.
Progress should never require abandoning those who helped build the present.
A society reveals its character not by how it treats the powerful, but by how it treats those whose voices are easiest to overlook.
I remain optimistic.
Not because I believe progress is automatic.
History tells us it is not.
Progress depends on participation.
It depends on people who refuse to believe that someone else will solve every problem.
It depends on citizens willing to contribute, to organize, to listen, and when necessary, to stand together.
That is the responsibility all of us share.
I began my career carrying tools into a factory.
Today I carry different responsibilities.
But my purpose has not changed.
I still work for the same people.
The welders.
The drivers.
The machine operators.
The technicians.
The cleaners.
The apprentices beginning their first jobs.
The experienced workers preparing for retirement.
The millions of men and women whose names rarely appear in newspapers, yet whose work keeps our economy moving every single day.
They do not ask for special treatment.
They ask for fairness.
They do not ask for charity.
They ask for opportunity.
And they do not ask anyone to build their future for them.
They simply ask to be given the chance to build it themselves.
As long as I have the privilege of representing them, that is exactly what I will continue fighting for.
Thank you.
