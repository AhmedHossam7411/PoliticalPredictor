Baseline Speech – Ambassador Nadia Ibrahim
Deputy Minister for International Affairs
"The Art of Building Bridges"
Good evening.
When people hear the word diplomat, they often imagine someone negotiating treaties in grand conference halls or shaking hands before cameras.
Those moments certainly exist.
But they represent only a very small part of diplomacy.
Most diplomacy happens quietly.
It happens in conversations that never become headlines.
It happens in meetings where success is measured not by dramatic announcements but by misunderstandings that never become conflicts.
Perhaps that is why diplomacy is sometimes difficult to explain.
People naturally notice crises.
They rarely notice the crises that were prevented.
After more than thirty years in public service, I have come to believe that leadership is often defined not by the problems we solve after they emerge, but by the relationships we build before those problems arise.
Relationships are, in many ways, a form of national infrastructure.
Like roads or bridges, they require continual maintenance.
Unlike roads or bridges, however, they are built through trust rather than concrete.
Trust grows slowly.
It weakens quickly.
And once damaged, it is rarely restored through words alone.
It must be rebuilt through consistent actions over time.
That lesson has remained with me throughout my career.
People sometimes assume diplomacy is simply about convincing others to agree with us.
I have never believed that.
Negotiation is not persuasion alone.
It begins with understanding.
You cannot effectively represent your own interests if you have not first taken the time to understand the interests of others.
Understanding does not require agreement.
It requires curiosity.
Those two ideas are often confused.
Early in my career, I attended an international conference where representatives from several countries spent hours repeating prepared statements.
Each delegation spoke clearly.
Each delegation defended its position confidently.
Yet very little progress was made.
At the end of the meeting, one senior diplomat quietly observed something I have never forgotten.
He said, "Everyone has spoken. Very few have listened."
That sentence has stayed with me for decades.
Listening is one of the most underestimated leadership skills.
Many people view listening as passive.
I see it as one of the most active forms of analysis.
When people speak, they communicate more than their official position.
They reveal priorities.
Concerns.
Assumptions.
Pressures.
Sometimes they even reveal opportunities for cooperation they themselves have not yet recognized.
If we listen carefully enough, disagreement often becomes more understandable.
Not necessarily smaller.
But clearer.
Clarity is valuable.
Confusion rarely produces good decisions.
Throughout my career I have represented my country in discussions involving trade, security, development, education, and regional cooperation.
The subjects changed.
The participants changed.
The circumstances changed.
Yet one lesson remained remarkably consistent.
Very few international questions are entirely one-sided.
Every country views the world through its own experiences.
Its own history.
Its own responsibilities.
Its own constraints.
Recognizing that reality does not weaken our own position.
It strengthens our ability to negotiate effectively.
The strongest negotiators are not those who speak the most.
They are those who understand the greatest number of perspectives without losing sight of their own.
National interest remains the responsibility of every public servant.
But national interest should never be confused with national isolation.
A nation grows stronger through constructive relationships, not through unnecessary distance.
Partnerships require confidence.
Confidence requires credibility.
Credibility requires consistency.
These principles apply equally to individuals and to governments.
One promise honored creates possibilities for future cooperation.
One promise broken creates uncertainty that extends far beyond a single agreement.
For that reason, reliability is one of the most valuable assets any institution can possess.
People often ask whether diplomacy has become more difficult.
In some ways, yes.
The pace of communication has accelerated dramatically.
Information now travels across the world in seconds.
Opinions form quickly.
Expectations change rapidly.
At the same time, many of the challenges we face have become increasingly interconnected.
Economic questions influence security.
Environmental issues influence migration.
Technological developments influence international law.
Health concerns influence global trade.
The boundaries separating policy areas have become less distinct.
This reality demands something from leaders.
It demands intellectual flexibility.
No single discipline, ministry, or institution possesses every answer.
Complex questions require cooperation across expertise.
That is not a weakness.
It is an acknowledgment of reality.
One characteristic I have come to value deeply is patience.
Patience is often misunderstood as hesitation.
I disagree.
Patience means remaining committed to long-term objectives even when immediate progress appears limited.
Diplomatic work sometimes advances in centimeters rather than kilometers.
Yet those centimeters often determine whether future generations inherit stability or conflict.
The public naturally celebrates decisive moments.
History, however, is often shaped by patient ones.
I have learned that trust cannot be demanded.
It must be demonstrated.
Respect cannot be requested.
It must be earned.
Influence cannot simply be declared.
It emerges when others believe your words will be followed by responsible actions.
These principles seem simple.
Applying them consistently is considerably more difficult.
Leadership frequently involves balancing competing responsibilities.
We must remain firm without becoming inflexible.
Open without becoming naïve.
Confident without becoming dismissive.
Protective of national interests while remaining receptive to international cooperation.
Balance is rarely dramatic.
It is rarely celebrated.
Yet it is often where effective leadership resides.
I have also learned that disagreement should never automatically become hostility.
Reasonable people may arrive at different conclusions because they begin from different experiences.
Recognizing those differences does not require abandoning our own principles.
Rather, it allows us to engage with greater precision.
It is much easier to solve a disagreement once its true source has been identified.
Throughout my career I have worked alongside remarkable colleagues from many different countries.
Different languages.
Different political systems.
Different traditions.
Despite those differences, I have consistently discovered something encouraging.
Most public servants enter government for remarkably similar reasons.
They wish to contribute.
They wish to improve conditions for their citizens.
They wish to leave their country stronger than they found it.
The methods may differ.
The priorities may differ.
The pace may differ.
But the underlying motivations often possess far more common ground than public debate sometimes suggests.
That realization has made me more optimistic.
Not because conflict has disappeared.
Conflict remains part of international affairs.
Always has been.
Always will be.
My optimism comes from observing what becomes possible when dialogue continues despite disagreement.
Communication keeps options open.
Silence rarely does.
One of the greatest responsibilities of leadership is preserving the possibility of future cooperation even during periods of profound disagreement.
Closing every door is easy.
Reopening them later is considerably harder.
That perspective has influenced my own approach to decision-making.
Before reaching conclusions, I try to ask several questions.
What information might we still be missing?
How might others interpret this decision?
What consequences extend beyond the immediate objective?
How will today's actions influence tomorrow's relationships?
These questions do not eliminate uncertainty.
They help manage it.
Public service has also taught me something about humility.
No leader sees the entire picture.
No institution possesses complete information.
No country controls every variable shaping the international environment.
Acknowledging these realities does not diminish leadership.
It strengthens judgment.
Confidence without humility often becomes overconfidence.
Humility without confidence becomes hesitation.
Effective leadership requires both.
As our world continues evolving, the importance of cooperation will only increase.
Artificial intelligence.
Cybersecurity.
Public health.
Climate resilience.
Global supply chains.
None of these issues can be addressed effectively by one institution acting entirely alone.
Interdependence is no longer simply an academic concept.
It is a practical reality.
Our challenge is not deciding whether we are connected.
Our challenge is deciding how wisely we manage those connections.
When young people ask me what diplomacy really is, I give them a very simple answer.
Diplomacy is the discipline of building understanding before misunderstanding becomes conflict.
Sometimes that work succeeds visibly.
More often, it succeeds quietly.
Both forms of success matter.
As I reflect upon my career, I find myself less interested in individual achievements than in enduring relationships.
Agreements eventually expire.
Governments change.
Leaders retire.
Circumstances evolve.
Relationships, when carefully cultivated, often outlast all of them.
Those relationships become the foundation upon which future generations solve problems we cannot yet imagine.
If our work today leaves behind stronger institutions, deeper trust, and greater habits of cooperation, then we will have accomplished something meaningful.
Not simply for our own generation.
But for those who inherit the world after us.
That, I believe, is the true purpose of public service.
Thank you.
