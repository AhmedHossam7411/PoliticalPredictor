Baseline Speech – Ibrahim Saleh
Chief Executive Officer, Horizon Capital Investment Fund
"Confidence: The Invisible Asset"
Good evening.
People often ask me what I do.
When I tell them I manage investments, the conversation usually moves in one of two directions.
Some people immediately ask where they should invest their money.
Others assume that my job is simply predicting the future.
Neither answer is quite right.
My profession has taught me something much more interesting.
Success rarely belongs to the people who predict the future most accurately.
It belongs to the people who prepare for several possible futures at the same time.
That distinction is subtle.
But it changes the entire way you think.
Most people imagine investing as a search for certainty.
I have spent my career learning to become comfortable with uncertainty.
Not because uncertainty is desirable.
Because it is unavoidable.
Markets rise.
Markets fall.
Technologies transform industries.
Governments change priorities.
Consumer preferences evolve.
Unexpected events occur with remarkable regularity.
The world has never promised stability.
The question has always been how wisely we respond to instability.
Very early in my career I believed my responsibility was finding the perfect investment.
After enough years, I realized perfect investments do not exist.
Every opportunity contains risk.
Every risk contains opportunity.
The objective is not eliminating uncertainty.
It is understanding it more clearly than others.
That realization fundamentally changed the way I make decisions.
Whenever someone presents an investment proposal, I rarely begin by asking how much money it could generate.
My first question is usually much simpler.
"What assumptions must be true for this to succeed?"
That single question often reveals more than an hour of enthusiastic presentations.
Good decisions are built upon assumptions.
Great decisions begin by examining those assumptions before committing resources.
One of the greatest mistakes intelligent people make is confusing confidence with certainty.
Confidence says, "Based on what we know, this is our best judgment."
Certainty says, "There is no possibility we are mistaken."
Experience has made me increasingly confident.
It has also made me considerably less certain.
The older I become, the more I appreciate how many variables influence every important outcome.
That realization has not weakened my willingness to make decisions.
It has improved the quality of those decisions.
Humility is surprisingly practical.
People often describe financial markets as emotional.
They are correct.
Markets are ultimately collections of human decisions.
Fear spreads.
Optimism spreads.
Panic spreads.
Confidence spreads.
The numbers displayed on trading screens represent something much deeper than mathematics.
They represent expectations.
Expectations about tomorrow.
When people believe tomorrow will be better, they invest.
When they become uncertain, they hesitate.
Confidence, therefore, becomes one of the most valuable economic resources any society can possess.
Curiously, confidence cannot simply be announced.
It must be earned.
Investors do not respond to speeches alone.
They observe consistency.
They examine incentives.
They compare actions with promises.
Trust develops gradually because credibility develops gradually.
That principle extends far beyond financial markets.
Families make long-term decisions when they feel secure.
Entrepreneurs hire employees when they believe the future is sufficiently predictable.
Researchers begin ambitious projects when institutions appear stable.
Confidence quietly influences almost every significant decision we make.
One lesson that investing teaches very quickly is that incentives matter more than intentions.
Most people have good intentions.
Intentions are important.
They are rarely sufficient.
Human beings generally respond to the incentives surrounding them.
If incentives reward long-term thinking, people usually think further ahead.
If incentives reward short-term behavior, short-term behavior becomes more common.
Designing effective systems therefore requires understanding human behavior rather than simply hoping people will behave differently.
Some misunderstand this perspective.
They assume thinking about incentives means ignoring ethics.
I believe the opposite.
Ethics become stronger when systems encourage ethical behavior rather than depending exclusively upon extraordinary individuals.
Good institutions reduce the cost of doing the right thing.
That may sound technical.
In reality, it is deeply human.
Leadership has also taught me that patience possesses enormous economic value.
The world often celebrates speed.
Move faster.
Expand faster.
Respond faster.
Sometimes that is appropriate.
Other times patience creates extraordinary advantages.
Capital compounds.
Knowledge compounds.
Trust compounds.
Reputation compounds.
These processes cannot be rushed.
Compounding rewards consistency far more than excitement.
People sometimes ask whether I become frustrated when markets behave irrationally.
Not particularly.
Human beings have always been imperfect decision-makers.
We overreact.
We underestimate certain risks while exaggerating others.
We become influenced by recent events.
Recognizing these tendencies allows us to design better decision-making processes.
That is why our organization spends considerable time questioning itself.
We actively search for information that contradicts our preferred conclusions.
If everyone around the table agrees immediately, I become uncomfortable.
Not because agreement is undesirable.
Because important risks may not yet have been identified.
Constructive disagreement protects organizations from overconfidence.
It is one of the healthiest investments any institution can make.
Throughout my career I have worked with entrepreneurs, engineers, teachers, physicians, manufacturers, scientists, government officials, and community organizations.
The sectors differ dramatically.
Yet one characteristic consistently predicts success.
Adaptability.
The strongest organizations are rarely those that resist change most effectively.
They are those that preserve their core principles while adapting their methods.
That balance requires discipline.
Every successful organization eventually faces a temptation.
To assume that yesterday's success guarantees tomorrow's.
History repeatedly demonstrates otherwise.
Complacency has destroyed more institutions than competition ever could.
Learning is therefore not merely desirable.
It is essential.
Curiosity is an economic asset.
Organizations that continue asking thoughtful questions remain resilient.
Organizations convinced they already possess every important answer gradually become vulnerable.
That lesson has shaped my own leadership philosophy.
I do not hire people because they always agree with me.
I hire people capable of improving my thinking.
The purpose of leadership is not surrounding oneself with admiration.
It is surrounding oneself with insight.
Sometimes the most valuable person in the room is the individual willing to respectfully identify the weakness everyone else overlooked.
I consider that loyalty, not opposition.
There is another concept that has become increasingly important to me over the years.
Optionality.
Many people believe strength comes from committing completely to a single path.
Sometimes it does.
Often strength comes from preserving the ability to adapt as circumstances evolve.
Maintaining options is not indecision.
It is strategic flexibility.
When uncertainty increases, flexibility becomes valuable.
Not because it predicts the future.
Because it acknowledges the future cannot be perfectly predicted.
One of the privileges of my profession is observing societies over long periods of time.
Economic success is rarely determined by one remarkable year.
It is usually determined by decades of consistent institutional improvement.
Strong legal systems.
Reliable education.
Sound infrastructure.
Responsible governance.
Healthy financial institutions.
None of these attract headlines every day.
Collectively they influence almost every opportunity that follows.
People occasionally ask me whether money motivates me.
It certainly matters.
Capital is an extraordinarily useful tool.
But tools are valuable because of what they allow us to build.
Not because they exist.
Financial resources create possibilities.
They support research.
Infrastructure.
Entrepreneurship.
Healthcare.
Education.
Culture.
The real question is never how much capital exists.
It is how wisely that capital is allocated.
Allocation reflects values.
Every investment says something about the future we believe is worth building.
That realization gives my work a greater sense of purpose.
As I reflect upon my career, I find myself thinking less about financial returns and more about institutional resilience.
Can organizations continue functioning during periods of stress?
Can they adapt without abandoning integrity?
Can they learn faster than circumstances change?
These questions matter because resilience creates opportunity.
Fragility destroys it.
The future will undoubtedly present challenges we cannot yet anticipate.
New technologies.
New industries.
New geopolitical realities.
New environmental pressures.
History guarantees that uncertainty will continue.
History also demonstrates something encouraging.
Human beings repeatedly adapt.
Not because adaptation is comfortable.
Because learning remains one of our greatest strengths.
The organizations that thrive in the coming decades will not necessarily be the largest.
They will be the ones that remain intellectually curious.
The ones willing to examine assumptions.
The ones capable of balancing confidence with humility.
And the ones that understand that trust, credibility, and thoughtful judgment are assets every bit as valuable as financial capital.
If there is one lesson I hope to leave with young leaders, it is this:
Do not spend your life searching for certainty.
Spend it building the judgment to act wisely when certainty is impossible.
Because leadership has never been about predicting every outcome.
It has always been about making sound decisions despite imperfect information.
If we learn to do that consistently, we will discover that the most valuable investment we can ever make is not merely in markets.
It is in our capacity to think clearly, learn continuously, and act with integrity.
Thank you.
