Baseline Speech – Karim Adel
Founder & CEO, NovaMind Technologies
"Building Tomorrow Before It Arrives"
Good afternoon.
People often ask me what it's like to work in technology.
I usually tell them they're asking the wrong question.
Technology isn't really an industry anymore.
It's becoming the language every industry speaks.
Whether you're a farmer using satellite imagery, a doctor relying on machine learning to detect disease, a manufacturer optimizing production with artificial intelligence, or a teacher reaching students through digital platforms, technology is no longer sitting on the sidelines.
It has become part of how we solve problems.
That is what excites me.
Not the software.
Not the hardware.
Not even artificial intelligence itself.
What excites me is human potential.
Technology is simply one of the tools we have invented to expand it.
I founded my company because I became tired of hearing people say, "That's impossible."
Whenever someone says those words, I hear something different.
I hear, "Nobody has solved it yet."
There is a significant difference.
History is full of ideas that sounded unrealistic right up until the moment someone made them ordinary.
Flying.
Instant communication.
Computers in our pockets.
Genetic medicine.
Electric vehicles.
At one point every one of those ideas sounded impossible.
Then someone decided that impossible was merely a temporary description.
Innovation has always belonged to people who are willing to challenge assumptions that others have accepted for too long.
I have always been fascinated by that process.
When I was a student, I wasn't interested in memorizing answers.
I wanted to understand why those answers existed in the first place.
Once you understand the reasoning behind something, you begin asking a different kind of question.
Not "How do I use this?"
But "How could this become better?"
That single shift in thinking has shaped almost every decision I've made.
People often assume entrepreneurs are gamblers.
I disagree.
Good entrepreneurs don't ignore risk.
We study it.
The stereotype is that entrepreneurs wake up one morning, have an idea, and suddenly launch a company.
Reality is much less glamorous.
You test.
You fail.
You rebuild.
You test again.
You discover that your customers don't actually have the problem you thought they had.
You redesign the product.
You discover another flaw.
You redesign again.
Most successful companies are built through thousands of adjustments that nobody outside the company ever notices.
Perfection rarely appears in version one.
That's okay.
Progress matters more than perfection.
One of the biggest differences between fast-moving organizations and slow-moving organizations isn't intelligence.
It's how they treat mistakes.
Some organizations punish mistakes so severely that people stop experimenting.
The result is predictable.
Innovation slows.
Creativity disappears.
Eventually, the organization becomes excellent at protecting yesterday's success instead of creating tomorrow's.
I tell every engineer who joins our company the same thing.
"If you're never wrong, you're probably not trying anything ambitious enough."
Now, that doesn't mean mistakes don't matter.
Of course they matter.
What matters even more is whether we learn from them quickly.
A mistake repeated ten times is no longer a mistake.
It's a management problem.
Speed is often misunderstood.
People think moving quickly means acting carelessly.
It doesn't.
Moving quickly means shortening the distance between learning and improving.
That's why startups often outperform much larger organizations.
It's not because startups have more resources.
Quite the opposite.
We simply cannot afford to spend twelve months debating something that could be tested in two weeks.
Action generates information.
Information improves decisions.
Waiting often produces neither.
Another misconception I hear frequently is that technology replaces people.
I don't think that's the right way to look at it.
Throughout history, every major technological revolution has changed work.
Some jobs disappeared.
New jobs emerged.
Skills evolved.
The important question has never been whether change happens.
The important question is whether society prepares people to succeed within that change.
Technology should amplify human capability, not diminish human dignity.
If innovation improves efficiency while leaving people behind, then we've only solved half the problem.
Real innovation improves lives.
Otherwise, it's just engineering.
I spend a great deal of time thinking about education.
Not because our company hires graduates.
Because education determines whether societies become creators of technology or merely consumers of it.
The difference is profound.
Consumers wait for the future.
Creators build it.
Every young person who learns to solve problems instead of simply memorizing information becomes a force multiplier for society.
That is why curiosity matters so much.
Curiosity cannot be automated.
Artificial intelligence can process enormous amounts of information.
It cannot replace the uniquely human ability to ask entirely new questions.
Questions create revolutions.
Answers refine them.
People sometimes ask me whether artificial intelligence worries me.
My answer usually surprises them.
Artificial intelligence itself doesn't concern me nearly as much as human complacency.
Technology develops whether we participate or not.
The real choice is whether we help shape its direction.
If responsible people refuse to engage with emerging technologies because they're uncomfortable, less responsible people will gladly fill the vacuum.
Leadership means participating early enough to influence outcomes.
Not complaining after decisions have already been made.
That philosophy applies well beyond technology.
Every generation inherits problems.
Every generation also inherits opportunities.
The difference often lies in perspective.
Some people see disruption.
Others see possibility.
Neither perspective is entirely wrong.
But if all you see are obstacles, you'll never discover opportunities hidden behind them.
Optimism is often misunderstood.
People assume optimism means believing everything will work out.
It doesn't.
Optimism means believing that effort still matters.
That we are not merely passengers watching history unfold.
That our choices influence outcomes.
Without that belief, innovation becomes impossible.
No entrepreneur starts a company believing that individual actions make no difference.
We build because we believe they do.
One lesson entrepreneurship taught me is that confidence and certainty are not the same thing.
I can be highly confident while admitting I don't know exactly what the market will look like five years from now.
Nobody knows.
The future has never been predictable.
It has only been buildable.
That distinction changes everything.
Instead of asking, "What will happen?"
I ask, "What can we help make happen?"
That question creates responsibility.
If our products influence millions of people, then we carry millions of responsibilities.
Technology companies cannot pretend they are simply writing code.
We shape communication.
Education.
Healthcare.
Commerce.
Relationships.
With influence comes accountability.
I welcome that responsibility.
Because avoiding responsibility has never produced better innovation.
Some people believe regulations automatically slow innovation.
Sometimes they do.
Sometimes they improve it.
Good rules create trust.
Trust encourages adoption.
Adoption allows innovation to scale.
The real objective should never be fewer rules or more rules.
It should be better rules.
Rules designed by people who understand both technology and society.
That balance isn't easy.
But difficult problems are precisely the reason innovators exist.
Another thing entrepreneurship has taught me is that talent is distributed far more evenly than opportunity.
I've interviewed brilliant software developers who never had access to world-class universities.
I've met extraordinary designers who taught themselves using free online resources.
Potential exists everywhere.
Opportunity does not.
One of the greatest responsibilities of leadership is expanding opportunity faster than inequality.
If we fail to do that, we waste human potential on a massive scale.
And wasted potential is perhaps the most expensive resource any society can lose.
People sometimes ask what success looks like for me.
It's not becoming the largest company.
It's not having the highest valuation.
Those achievements are gratifying, but they're temporary.
Success is creating products that become so useful people stop thinking about them.
Electricity transformed civilization.
Most of us rarely think about electricity anymore.
That's what truly successful innovation does.
It quietly becomes part of everyday life.
I also measure success by the people who leave our company.
That may sound strange.
But if someone spends five years here, learns how to build remarkable things, and then starts their own company, I don't consider that a loss.
I consider it multiplication.
Innovation ecosystems don't grow because one company becomes dominant.
They grow because knowledge spreads.
Ideas evolve.
People build upon one another's successes.
Competition doesn't weaken innovation.
It accelerates it.
As long as it remains fair.
As I look toward the future, I don't see a world divided between humans and machines.
I see a world that increasingly rewards adaptability.
The ability to learn.
To question assumptions.
To collaborate across disciplines.
To embrace change without abandoning ethics.
Technology will continue evolving.
That much is certain.
The question is whether our institutions, our education systems, and our leadership evolve with it.
I believe they can.
History gives us every reason to believe they will.
Human beings have always adapted.
Not perfectly.
Not immediately.
But remarkably.
Every generation inherits challenges previous generations could barely imagine.
And somehow, through creativity, persistence, and collaboration, people continue finding solutions.
That gives me enormous confidence.
Not confidence in technology.
Confidence in people.
Technology is simply one more expression of what human beings have always done best.
We imagine futures that don't yet exist.
Then we build them.
Thank you.
